Simulation studies · 2026

Client work stays confidential, so these are independent simulation studies on a synthetic grocery retailer: 120 products, 104 weeks and 6,000 loyalty customers. Because the simulator knows the true answers, every method is scored against ground truth, and each study says where it would break on real data.

Simulation studyPricing

Shelf prices that earn more without spooking shoppers

92%

of the best possible margin gain, from noisy data, inside a ±10% price limit.

Most products change shelf price only a few times, so per-product elasticities are too noisy to price from. Shrinking them toward their category, then optimising under guardrails, gets close to what perfect knowledge would deliver.

Empirical BayesSLSQPElasticityPython
Read the case study 3 min

Recommendation

Price from shrunk elasticities, cap moves at ±10% and hold the category price index at most 2% higher. That gives +4.8% margin against true demand, with revenue within 1%.

Approach

Elasticities come from clean regular-price weeks, with promo, post-promo and out-of-stock weeks excluded. They are shrunk toward the category mean in proportion to their standard error, using a robust (MAD) estimate of spread. Then SLSQP runs per category under margin, revenue and price-index constraints.

Validation

  • Plans are scored on the simulator's true demand: raw +3.9%, shrunk +4.8%, oracle +5.3%.
  • Median absolute elasticity error falls from 0.45 to 0.25. 9% of raw estimates have the wrong sign, against none after shrinkage.

Where this breaks

Cross-price effects aren't in the optimiser, and real shelf moves aren't random: they follow costs and competitors. On live data I'd use cost-pass-through moves as instruments and fit a full hierarchical model.

How I'd test it in market

Pair stores on 26 weeks of pre-period sales and randomise within pairs. Run for 8 weeks and read margin with a CUPED adjustment, with the store count sized from pre-period variance. Stop early if price-perception tracking drops.

Simulation studyPromotions

Which promotions actually make money

10%

off is the only depth that adds margin on average. Every deeper unfunded discount loses it.

Gross uplift counts sales taken from sister products and from next week. Decomposing 1,488 promotions shows a quarter of promo volume isn't new to the business.

Demand modellingCannibalisationPull-forwardRidge
Read the case study 3 min

Recommendation

Cap unfunded depth at 10% and pair it with display, which adds 65% more net units per event. Deeper cuts only make sense with supplier funding or a traffic goal.

Approach

A log-linear demand model per product with seasonality, price, promo, display, a post-promo dip and a subcategory cannibalisation term. Each event is split into gross, cannibalised, pulled-forward and net units, and margin also charges the discount given on baseline units.

Validation

  • Holdout WAPE of 13.3%, against 30.6% for the naive mean.
  • Recovered parameters correlate with the truth: cannibalisation r = 0.91, elasticity r = 0.78.

The funding assumption

82% of promotions lose margin when the retailer pays the whole discount. With 30% supplier funding that falls to 70%, and with 50% funding to 52%. The verdict depends on the deal, which is why it's a parameter.

Where this breaks

The model has the same form as the simulator, and promos here are placed at random. Real promos are chosen for season and supplier money. I'd test with a deliberately misspecified simulator and use promo-calendar instruments.

Simulation studyAssortment

A range review that keeps the sales

−19%

revenue lost when delisting 18 SKUs, compared with cutting the lowest sellers.

"Cut the tail" removes products whose shoppers have nowhere else to go. Out-of-stock weeks show how much demand moves to sister products, which reveals the safe delists.

Natural experimentsDemand transferShrinkage
Read the case study 2 min

Recommendation

Rank delist candidates by revenue × (1 − transfer rate), not by revenue alone, and keep at least 3 SKUs per subcategory.

Approach

In 254 out-of-stock weeks, compare how much sister products over-sold with what the missing product would have sold. Both sides come from the demand model, so promo weeks are usable. Rates are shrunk toward the subcategory.

Validation

Revenue lost: lowest sellers 2.17%, transfer-aware 1.76%, oracle 1.45%, which closes 57% of the gap. Transfer-rate error (MAE) falls from 0.21 to 0.18.

Where this breaks

Chain-level data is noisy per product. Customer-level switching from loyalty baskets is the stronger signal, and the next step.
